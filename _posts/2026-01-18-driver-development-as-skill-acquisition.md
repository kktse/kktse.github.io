Motorsport places significant effort into maximizing the vehicle potential.

From a vehicle dynamics perspective, the problem imposed by racing is to
produce the vehicle with the maximum acceleration capabilities and with the
control and stability characteristics for a skilled driver to operate[^1].
While it is standard practice to abstract away the the details of the driver,
the total problem imposed by racing is a closed-loop control problem.

The concept of the driver-athlete is a relatively new area of study. Racing
drivers face the stereotype that they are not athletes [^2]. Only in 2012 did
Potkanowicz et al. present the case that the racing driver is an athlete,
giving rise to the field of _driver science_.

_Driver science_ is playing a bigger role in professional motorsport.
Professional racing drivers routinely share their physical, perceptual and
cognitive conditioning routines on social media, further highlighting how
athletic development is becoming pervasive in the sport.

The highest levels of competition receive the most scientific inquiry. This
leaves the amateur and aspiring racing driver without the knowledge and
resources in their journey in become skillful. Because of this, there is an
opportunity to develop curriculum and pedagogy at these levels.

With a background primarily in vehicle dynamics, I wanted to understand how
contemporary frameworks of athlete development are used in other sports, and
examine how they might apply to driver development. This is a summary of
findings that are relevant to my goals in supporting the racing driver at the
amateur and grassroots level.

## Cultural context

Motorsport is a technology driven sport. Racing teams are results-oriented;
they have a keen focus on outcomes within the sport and in business. Teams rely
on data and facts to make decisions. [^3]

This extends to the amateur level. The aspiring racing driver must carefully
consider cost and competitiveness to achieve their desired goals.

Competency as a racing driver is demonstrated by superior performance in
competition. This is evident in social media where credibility is gained
through victory. The need-to-win attitude suggests an ego oriented motivational
climate where achievement is measured by podium finishes, championship wins and
the prestige that comes with it. [^4]

This motivational climate may be challenging for new learners who are just
beginning. Competition does not always provide objective feedback or relative
performance to themselves. Drivers who continually chase lap times or victories
in the absence of skill are at higher risk of negative psychological outcomes
like being dissatisfied or unmotivated.

This makes motorsport a challenging environment for coaches to operate in. It
is necessary to work within the competitive environment of the sport while
simultaneously creating a motivational climate that is supportive of the
driver's development.

## Nature of expertise

Experts differ from their novice counterparts in their superior ability to
perceive meaningful patterns. Expertise in one domain does not guarantee
expertise in another, suggesting that expertise is domain specific. [^5][^6]

For the amateur or aspiring racing driver, performance does not come from
innate talent or skill. Rather, skill comes from a superior ability to perceive
and act on task relevant information built up upon hours of practice. When
viewed from this perspective, driver development can be discussed in the
context of _learning_ and _expertise_.

## Traditional approach to coaching

The prevailing coaching strategy is based on traditional motor learning
frameworks, which state that expert motor control emerges from internalized
control processes [^7]. This is also known as information processing (IP)
theory. Learning is an internal process whereby skill emerges from superior
motor programming and automaticity.

In a document analysis of professional racing techniques, Lappi analyzes
examples of _deliberate practice_ and maps them to a control hierarchy
representing the driving task. Navigation is represented by a need establish a
trajectory and waypoints of a given race track, guidance is represented by a
need to perceive visual information and to select the corresponding series of
motor sequences, and control is represented by multisensory integration and the
stabilization of motor routines. [^8]

Internal representations can be interpreted through the lens of classical
schema theory by understanding them as a graph of base action concepts (BAC).
Schack et al. elicits representations by asking participants to perform a
series of pairwise comparisons of task-related concepts. Dendrograms are
compared between expert and non-expert groups. Differences in clustering reveal
how BACs are organized. Experts are better able to cluster concepts in a way
that aligns with their physical performance. [^9]

While this provides a theoretical basis for instruction, such theories suggest
a fixed form of expertise exists. The elicitation of expert schemas in practice
is limited, leaving it to the coach to determine what expert schemas look like.

## The racing driver is not a robot

This traditional theory motor learning is reflected in current coaching
practice. Data is compared ad nauseam, inspecting every little difference in
the telemetry with the assumption that simply reproducing one driver's inputs
will cause the emergence of skill in another. We simulate and optimize the
vehicle using numerical models of ever-increasing fidelity in the pursuit of
superior performance with the assumption that simulation
translates to reality.

Despite the technical focus of the sport, the racing driver is an
experience-based subjective performer. While technical experts may possess
superior _knowledge about_ the vehicle behaviour or the optimal trajectory, the
skill of a racing driver is governed by the implicit, procedural _knowledge of_
the driving task [^8]. In other words, the racing driver is not a
line-following robot.

In the wise words of a race engineer:

> To be the race engineer is to be the psychologist for the racing driver.

## Ecological dynamics approach to coaching

In contrast to frameworks that locate skills within the athlete's internal
cognitive process, ecological dynamics states that skill emerges from the
interaction between the individual and the task environment. In other words,
skill does not exist in isolation - it is a property of the
person-environment-task system itself. [^7][^10]

Improvement is a result of becoming _attuned_ to task relevant information from
within environment. The rich, perceptual information invites or _affords_ the
athlete to take action. The person-environment-task system is always evolving.
It is up to the athlete make functional adaptations to complete the task. [^10]

This perspective of skill acquisition therefore suggests that expertise is not
just from derived the athlete's experience, but also from their ability to
develop rapid, functional _adaptations_ as a result of their relationship with
the environment. [^10]

## Constraints-led approach

The ecological dynamics approach asks us not what is in the head of the
athlete, but what head of the athlete is in. By acknowledging the mutual
relationship between the individual and environment, coaches facilitate
learning by manipulating the environment through _constraints_. [^11][^12]

The constraints-led approach (CLA) is a pedagogic methodology within the
ecological dynamics framework. Coaches induce adaptation by introducing task or
environmental constraints, perturbing the current movement pattern to encourage
exploration of alternate solutions. [^11]

Rather than prescribing a fixed movement pattern, coaches invites the athlete
to solve specific motor problems. Constraints can include _environmental
constraints_, which can consist of physical constraints related to when and
where the task is being performed, socio-cultural constraints related to
expectations and values, and _task constraints_ which includes, but is not
limited to modifications to rules, goals, and equipment.

In a motorsport context, the manipulation of vehicle setup is a task constraint
since the vehicle performance constrains the solution and affords different
control strategies.

## Cultural challenges

The competitive nature of the sport combined with longstanding cultural beliefs
creates a challenging environment for coaches to be effective. Some of these
beliefs can be incongruent to effective coaching.

The so-called "driver modification" demands of drivers to change themselves
over making any modification the vehicle. The vehicle is an major component of
the sport and is nearly always modified to gain a competitive advantage
regardless of driver skill. From a coaching perspective, vehicle modification
is a valid constraint.

Adjacent to this is an implied belief that expert performance exists in
isolation of the environment. This manifests itself in prescriptive
turn-by-turn instruction where task repetition is performed in isolation of the
environment. Factors such as weather, track evolution, vehicle evolution, and
driver fatigue are not directly controllable, and thus require adaptation by
the driver to achieve the same outcomes.

## Discussion

Driver development at the amateur and grassroots level exists to support motor
learning in driver‑athletes.

Motorsport demands that drivers expertly and continuously integrate
multisensory information while working with technical staff to exploit the
vehicle's potential. The coach's role is to systematically contextualize the
evolving performance, environmental and competitive landscape to design
interventions that helps the driver make functional adaptations towards theirs
goals. Having a theoretical framework provides the basis for effective
coaching.

The information processing framework already benefits from a vast library of
driver and vehicle models to inform optimal control patterns. The McRuer
hierarchy is the standard model to represent the driving task, consisting of
guidance, navigation and control [^8]. This model can be used to locate where
in the driving task the inefficient motor pattern emerges so the root cause can
addressed.

Ecological dynamics is a contemporary theory of motor learning that places
athletic skill in the performer’s ability to perceive and act on information
through their relationship with the environment. This aligns with drivers'
subjective experience as experiential experts. Instructional design based upon
the ecological dynamics framework, such as the constraints-led approach or the
games-based approach, is a new frontier for coaches, offering an opportunity to
meet drivers where they are and to problem solve towards common goals.

Quality instruction should to move beyond prescriptive in-vehicle instruction
to better reflect contemporary understanding of motor learning. This requires
challenging fundamental concepts in existing curriculum. Take for instance the
racing line. In optimal control studies of a hairpin turn, both the optimal
trajectory and the required driver inputs change as the tire characteristics
change on different road surfaces [^15]. In an optimal control study of a
formula car around Circuit de Catalunya, optimizing the vehicle parameters
varied the racing line by over half a track width in some corners [^16].
Coaches must understand that the racing line changes as the relationship
between driver and environment changes. Coaches should encourage their athletes
to explore various racing lines in an effort to better attune to the prevailing
conditions. The timescale of the functional adaptations also changes depending
on the competitive context. For new learners, exploration facilitates problem
solving as they develop robust movement patterns. For expert performers,
adaptations are used to exploit the performance environment the day of
competition.

While current discussion in the scientific community presents ecological
dynamics and information processing as competing frameworks, coaches should be
respected as practitioners who apply these frameworks in the field. As a
results-oriented sport, the motorsports instructor must balance cultural
acceptability with effective instruction. Introducing a new coaching framework
must be done in a way that yields results to gain credibility and trust.
However, knowing how to apply the appropriate theory is a differentiator for
the coach in the quality of instruction and for their students in their ability
to perform in competition.

## In practice

As a vehicle dynamicist, the primary focus is on improving the performance
potential of the vehicle. However, the closed-loop nature of the sport
necessitates the need to consider the driver-vehicle system as a whole. In my
practice, my relationship with the driver comprises of two responsibilities.

The first responsibility is to intervene when inefficient motor patterns
emerge. Interventions are wide reaching and are not limited to driver skill.
For instance, we found that nutritional planning improved mood, reduced
subjective feelings of fatigue, and reduced the time to recovery after an
event. In more a conventional example, we found performance outcomes in
beginners improved by having them use a "steering with the head" visual gaze
strategy found in professional racing drivers [^17][^18].

With more experienced drivers, the responsibility shifts to a collaborative
relationship where we work together to solve problems. This approach respects
the driver-athlete as an expert performer and respects their autonomy to
self-organize. This strategy involves challenging their assumptions about the
performance environment. The goal is to help the driver attune to task relevant
information so they can make functional adaptations. This can be as simple as
prompting them to explain _how_ they plan on achieving their goals. Based on
this discussion, both parties can apply their expertise to improve performance,
whether that be through a change in control strategy made by the driver or
through a setup change on the vehicle.

## Conclusion

Motorsport is a multi‑disciplinary sport at the intersection of engineering and
human performance.

Technical exploitation of the vehicle has a long‑established history of
scientific inquiry, producing ever‑more capable machines that power the sport.

Every racing driver is an athlete, regardless of skill level. Contemporary
theories in cognitive science is a new frontier for improving outcomes in
driver-athletes. The benefits extend beyond performance to the broader safety
and accessibility of the sport.

Motorsport is, at its core, a sport. Significant effort is placed on maximizing
the vehicle potential. However, behind the racecar sits a human in the driver's
seat, expertly maneuvering through the environment to cross the finish line.
Driver development is the link between theory and practice and deserves the
same level of technical inquiry as the vehicle. As the sport makes advancements
in technology, so too should the driving instructor. The opportunity is clear -
by improving the instructional pedagogy, we can improve outcomes for racing
drivers of all levels, helping them achieve their goals and realize their
potential.
